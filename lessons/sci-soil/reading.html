About 16 minutes. Goal: order the particles and say why clay holds water.

Why this matters

Soil texture is how much sand, silt, and clay are in a sample. That mix changes water and nutrients. A mahiʻai uses it to choose the crop.

The sizes, not to scale

Sand
Silt
Clay

The picture shows relative size only. It is not to scale. The lesson uses these ranges: sand is 2 mm down to 0.05 mm, silt is 0.05 mm down to 0.002 mm, and clay is smaller than 0.002 mm.

A human hair is about 0.05 to 0.10 mm thick, similar to sand.

A spider web is about 0.002 to 0.003 mm, similar to silt.

A speck of cornstarch is about 0.001 mm, similar to clay.

Worked example

Large particles leave large spaces, so water moves through sand. Silt holds a middle amount. Clay particles are so small that water does not pass easily, which is why clay holds water.

About 18 minutes. Goal: name a texture from a ribbon, and say what a longer ribbon means.

Why this matters

A mahiʻai can feel soil texture in the field. The ribbon method needs no lab. More clay makes a longer ribbon, and more clay holds more water.

Worked example

  1. Wet a lump about the size of a golf ball until it holds together. If it drips, squeeze out the extra water.
  2. If the lump falls apart, the texture is sandy.
  3. If it holds, push a ribbon over your bent forefinger with your thumb.

Longer than 2 inches, very sticky: clayey.

From 1 inch to 2 inches: clay loam.

Holds its shape but makes no ribbon: silty loam.

Falls apart: sandy.

About 18 minutes. Goal: read a permeability rate and choose soil for wetland kalo.

Why this matters

Permeability is how easily liquid passes through something. A mahiʻai uses it to decide which kalo to plant. When water has a hard time getting through, it sits on top. Wetland kalo grows best surrounded by water.

Worked example

Slower than 0.2 inches in an hour: clayey.

From 0.2 to 6 inches in an hour: silty.

Faster than 6 inches in an hour: sandy.

Sand lets water through, so nutrients can pass with it. Clay holds water, and it can pond. Silt is the middle and is the usual fit for many plants. The classroom also times a tube for 10 minutes. The chart below is already in inches per hour, which is the unit in the lesson.

About 15 minutes. Goal: classify a percent and say what it means for water.

Why this matters

A loʻi for wetland kalo needs flowing water. Mahiʻai sent stream water in and then on to the next loʻi because a valley has slope. Too much slope and the water leaves before the crop drinks. Too little slope and the water can drown a crop that does not want to sit wet. Dryland kalo is the second case.

Worked example

Up to 8 percent: slight.

8 percent through 15 percent: moderate.

More than 15 percent: extreme. The field sheet also calls this a severe effect.

Slope here is rise over run, written as a percent. A physical slope finder is a board, a string, and a weight. You sight along two pins. This page uses the percents. You do not need the board.

An extreme slope erodes. Terraces slow that. On flat land, ponding is the problem. Irrigation can move the water, or the crop can be one that likes standing water.

About 14 minutes. Goal: sort three observations and name one job of soil organisms.

Why this matters

Soil organisms speed decay. Dead plants and animals become nutrients a crop can use. A loʻi with soil organisms is more productive than a loʻi without them.

Worked example

Insect: a small animal with jointed legs.

Fungus: white, fuzzy, thread-like, or mold-like growth.

Worms: the worksheet's other group. An earthworm tunnels, which opens air spaces so the soil is less packed and water can drain.

You do not need a microscope or a soil sample. Use the descriptions.

About 16 minutes. Goal: choose soil texture, water, and slope for one piece of land.

Why this matters

The three tests are one farming decision. Soil texture, slope, and soil organisms change whether a crop grows and whether the work pays.

Worked example

You inherit a large piece of land from your uncle. You want to grow organic plants, and you need them to grow efficiently.

For most plants, silty soil is the useful middle. Sandy soil holds too little water. Clay holds so much that some plants drown. It can also depend on the crop.

Irrigation is water people add so crops can grow. Sandy soil usually needs clay or other organic material. Clay soil usually needs sand or other larger particles. Silty soil usually needs no change.

A slight slope may need irrigation to drain. A moderate slope may need no drain, and it may need help holding water. A severe slope needs the land altered so water stays.
