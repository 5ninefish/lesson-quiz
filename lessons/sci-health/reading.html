About 15 minutes. Goal: connect heart disease in Hawaiʻi to the signals you can measure.

Why this matters

Heart disease kills more people in Hawaiʻi than anything else. The lesson’s sources say signs can begin in childhood, and that a large share of those deaths can be prevented by diet and exercise. Native Hawaiian death rates from heart disease were reported at nearly 1.7 times the rate for Whites living in Hawaiʻi.

Teaching

The ancient pattern in this lesson was active work, such as fishing, farming, and weaving, and a diet of mostly plants. A 1991 paper from the Waiʻanae Coast Comprehensive Health Center described that pattern as kalo, ʻuala, a smaller share of iʻa, and a still smaller share of puaʻa. An anthropologist in 1917 wrote that Native Hawaiians then had some of the strongest health on record. The lesson contrasts that with later imported processed food and more time spent sitting.

Vital signs are body temperature, pulse (your heart rate), and respiration (your breathing rate). Blood pressure is not always called a vital sign, and clinicians still measure it with them. Your own usual numbers, your baseline, matter. A single reading that is far from your baseline is a reason to pay attention, not a diagnosis on this page.

Worked example

A family names poi and sweet potato as everyday foods, and hula as the practice that keeps people moving. Those are examples, not the only right answers. Your food and your practice can be different.

About 15 minutes. Goal: write a hypothesis you can test with your own counts.

Why this matters

The scientific method in this lesson is a list you can follow: name a problem, ask a question, form a hypothesis, run the test, collect the data, analyze it, and state a conclusion. A hypothesis is an educated guess, usually written if-then.

Teaching

The thing you change is the independent variable. Here that is the activity: sitting, standing, or moving. The thing you measure is the dependent variable: heart rate, breathing rate, or blood pressure. Sitting quietly is the baseline. A control group in a bigger study does not get the treatment. This lesson does not use a separate control group. It compares conditions on the same person.

Worked example

If I march in place, then my breathing rate will rise. Activity is the independent variable. Breathing rate is the dependent variable. “I feel bored in the morning” is an opinion, not a hypothesis.

About 18 minutes. Goal: record a sitting baseline.

Why this matters

You measure what you want to manage. The sitting numbers are the baseline the later conditions get compared with.

Teaching

Heart rate, or pulse, is how many times the heart beats in one minute. The lesson cites a usual resting range of about 60 to 80 beats per minute, and people differ. Count beats for 15 seconds with two fingers on the thumb side of your wrist, then this page multiplies by four.

Respiration is breaths per minute. One breath is one inhale and one exhale, one rise and fall of the chest. Count for a full quiet minute. Adults at rest are often between 12 and 20 breaths per minute.

Systolic is the top number: pressure while the heart beats. Diastolic is the bottom number: pressure while the heart refills. The lesson cites an adult reading under 120 and under 80 as the normal range named by the American Heart Association in 2015. For teens, a high reading depends on age, height, and gender. Do not decide you have hypertension from this page.

Worked example

Someone counts 18 beats in 15 seconds. The heart rate is 72. They count 14 chest rises in one quiet minute. The cuff stays blank because they do not have one.

About 22 minutes. Goal: repeat the counts while standing and after movement.

Why this matters

The classroom experiment changes one thing, the activity, and measures the same signals again. The conditions are standing, after 2 minutes of movement, and after 3 minutes. Running in place is the classroom version. March, or lift a book about ten times, if you should not run.

Stop if you feel dizzy, have chest pain, or cannot speak in a sentence. This is a science count, not a workout test and not a diagnosis.

Worked example

Example only, not your body: sitting 72 beats and 14 breaths, standing 84 and 16, after marching 112 and 22. The count starts as soon as the movement stops, because waiting lets the number fall.

About 15 minutes. Goal: decide what your numbers say, and spot a bad measurement.

Why this matters

Reliability means a method gives stable results. If someone else followed your steps, they should land near the same number. Validity means the method measures the thing you claim it measures. A count can be repeated and still be the wrong count.

Worked example

Jo measured her heart rate once. Stacy measured it on five mornings. Kathy measured it on ten mornings. Kathy’s method is the most reliable because it was repeated. A different group counted breaths and wrote the number in the heart-rate box. Repeating that mistake would be reliable and still not valid.

About 12 minutes. Goal: turn the experiment into a note someone in your family could use.

Why this matters

Personal counts do not explain every difference in heart health. The classroom lesson also asks about cost, distance, time, access to care, and loss of traditional health knowledge. You do not have to share your family’s private medical history.

Teaching

If a heart rate or breathing rate is high because you are stressed, the lesson’s school steps are to pause, talk with a teacher or counselor, and slow down. Seeking care is the step when something stays wrong. People the lesson names as healers include lāʻau lapaʻau practitioners Leinaʻala Bright and Keoki Kīkaha Pai Baclayon, and Dr. Richard Kekuni Akana Blaisdell. Those names are examples of paths, not a quiz answer.
