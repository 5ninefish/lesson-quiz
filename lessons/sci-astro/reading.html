Why this sky lesson is here

People in Hawaiʻi have used the sky for navigation, farming, and fishing. The hōkūleʻa worldwide voyage used the stars to cross the ocean. The mahiʻai, the farmer, used moon phases to choose planting, weeding, or harvesting. The lawaiʻa, the fisher, used moon phases to choose onshore or deep-sea fishing. Later in the lesson, light itself becomes the evidence: a spectrum can name the elements in a lamp or in a star.

About 12 minutes. Goal: name a celestial object, explain daytime glare, and sort star, planet, and meteor.

Why this matters

A celestial object is something you can find in the sky. The list includes satellites, moons, comets, meteors, planets, and stars. Airplanes, satellites, and meteors move quickly across the sky. Most of the light you see from space comes from stars, or from light those stars reflect. Planets and moons in our solar system do not make their own light.

Worked example

Imagine a dark room. A flashlight dot on the wall is easy to see. Turn the room lights on and keep the flashlight on. The same dot is hard to see. The flashlight did not go away. The brighter light hid it. Stars are in the sky in the daytime too. Sunlight’s glare hides them. They become easier to see at night.

A star can twinkle. Light reflected from a planet does not twinkle; a planet shines with a steady light. A meteor can streak across the sky and glow as it burns. The Sun is a star, and it is the closest star to Earth. Earth goes around the Sun.

As Earth spins, the stars appear to move. In the northern sky, Polaris, the North Star, barely moves through the night. The other northern stars appear to wheel around it. Light pollution, city lights, and dirty air make the night sky harder to read.

About 15 minutes. Goal: use right ascension and declination, and compare two eyepieces.

Why this matters

Right ascension and declination are coordinates for objects in the sky. They work like longitude and latitude on Earth. Astronomers use the two numbers to point a telescope. A date belongs with those numbers, because Earth is turning and the sky you see changes.

Worked example

Right ascension is like longitude. As Earth spins, new stars rise along the eastern horizon. It is measured in hours, with 24 hours in a full turn, then minutes and seconds. Declination is like latitude. Stars near the poles follow smaller circles. Stars near the equator follow larger circles. Declination is measured in degrees: 90 degrees is straight north, −90 degrees is south, and 0 degrees is the equator.

A refractor telescope uses two glass lenses. A reflector telescope uses mirrors. The objective lens is the large lens at the front. It has a long focal length, gathers more light than a pupil, and makes a small upside-down image. The eyepiece is the small lens you look through. It has a short focal length and magnifies that image. A finder scope helps you aim. The focus knob sharpens an image that is already in view. It cannot make an empty field suddenly contain a star.

Magnification is the objective lens focal length divided by the eyepiece focal length. Sample numbers, not a claim about a classroom telescope: an objective lens of 900 mm and an eyepiece of 20 mm give 900 ÷ 20 = 45. A smaller eyepiece focal length gives a higher magnification and a narrower view. Star clusters are wide, so a lower magnification shows more of them. Planets are small, so a higher magnification helps. Never look at the Sun through a telescope.

Distance is a different question from position. An astronomical unit is a distance unit used inside the solar system. Right ascension and declination name a direction on the sky, not how far away the object is.

About 18 minutes. Goal: order the four star families and connect one of them to its season.

Why this matters

The night sky changes through the year as Earth goes around the Sun. The groups of stars that take turns dominating that sky are called Nā ʻOhana Hōkū ʻEhā, the four star families. A star line is the figure people remember inside one family. Navigators such as Nainoa Thompson use more horizon tools than the four pictures here. These figures are the simplified set from the lesson.

Worked example

The student summary names them in this order:

  1. Kekāomakaliʻi, the Bailer of Makaliʻi. Winter into spring. A curve, like a bailer or a bowl.
  2. Kaiwikuamoʻo, the Backbone. Spring into summer. A line, sometimes called the north-south star line.
  3. Mānaiakalani, the fishhook of Maui. Summer into fall. A triangle. The worksheet also calls this family the Chief’s Fishline. It is the same family.
  4. Kalupeakawelo, the Kite of Kawelo. Fall into winter. A square. The worksheet also prints Ka Lupe o Kawelo.

Nainoa Thompson’s way to remember the sequence is geometric: a curve, then a line, then a triangle, then a square. The same memory as objects: a bailer, a backbone, a fishhook, a kite. The families appear one after another. An app can show a constellation turned compared with a worksheet. That does not change which family comes next in the year. About 12 minutes. Goal: explain why solar time and clock time differ.

Why this matters

The sky is also a clock and a calendar. In ʻōlelo Hawaiʻi, wā, manawa, and au can all name a stretch of time. They are not the same idea as a numbered hour.

Worked example

Clock time cuts a day into equal hours, minutes, and seconds. Those hours stay the same length all year. A clock can read 12:00 when the Sun is not at its highest.

Solar time follows the Sun. Awakea is the middle of the day, when the Sun is highest, not a numbered hour that means the same thing on every clock. Before contact, people in Hawaiʻi did not split the day into hours, minutes, and seconds. They named parts of the day: kakahiaka for morning, awakea for noon, and pilipuka for midnight to dawn. Pō is nighttime. Lā or ao is daytime.

Makahiki is the year and an annual festival. It follows the Moon, so it is a lunar calendar, not a sun-based year like most western calendars. Mahina means the month or the moon and lasts about 29.5 days. Each month was split into three anahulu of about 10 days: the moon growing, the moon full or mostly full, and the moon shrinking. The moon looks full on Akua, Hoku, and Māhealani. Moon phases come from where Earth, the Moon, and the Sun are. The Sun lights the face of the Moon that points toward it.

About 15 minutes. Goal: separate visible light, refraction, and a matching pair of spectra.

Why this matters

Astronomers learn what something is made of by reading its light. You only see a small part of the light that exists.

Worked example

Visible light is the light you can see. Its wavelengths are about 400 to 700 nanometers, and it holds the colors of a rainbow. Other forms of light are real and invisible here: radio waves, microwaves, infrared, ultraviolet, and x-rays. Together they are the electromagnetic spectrum. They are all light, arranged by wavelength and frequency.

Color comes from wavelength. A long ʻukulele string makes a long wave and a low note. A short string makes a short wave and a high note. Light works in that same spirit: a different wavelength is a different color. Sunlight looks white, and it is made of all those colors.

Refraction is light changing direction because it enters a new material. Different wavelengths bend by different amounts, so a prism, or a raindrop, can unmix white light into a rainbow. A rainbow is the spectrum of the Sun. Spectrum means the range of wavelengths in a light. Different stars can have different spectra.

Every element has a spectrum that matches it. An absorption spectrum is the range of wavelengths that element removes from light passing through it. An emission spectrum is the range it gives off when energy is put into it. For the same element, those two sets of wavelengths match. Spectrometry is the study of spectra. About 20 minutes. Goal: order a spectroscope build, and tell sunlight from a compact fluorescent lamp.

Why this matters

A spectroscope lets you see a spectrum. You will not cut cardboard or peel a DVD in this preview. You will still learn the build, the safety rule, and what two real lamps look like.

Worked example

The classroom build uses a printed card, tape, and a piece of DVD-R. A diffraction grating is a set of very close lines that spread light. The clear layer of a DVD-R can be that grating.

  1. Cut along the outer edge and fold the card. Labels stay on the outside. Tape the flaps down, except the door that will hold the grating.
  2. Split a DVD-R into quarters, peel off the shiny layer, and cut a small clean square from the clear layer. Fingerprints and scratches ruin it.
  3. Tape the square inside the door, around the piece rather than over it, with the curved side facing out. The grating lines run up and down, so the rainbow runs sideways.
  4. Point the slit at a lamp and look through the grating, a little down or to the side rather than straight through. Do not look at the Sun, a laser, or another bright light.

Sunlight’s visible light is a continuous rainbow: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet, blended rather than chopped into separate bright lines. A compact fluorescent lamp shows separate bright lines. Those lines are the mercury emission spectrum. An incandescent bulb is closer to a continuous glow. An LED can be only a few colors, such as red and blue. Hold and angle change what you see, but they do not turn mercury’s separate lines into the Sun’s continuous band.

Firefighters use a spectroscope on smoke to look for dangerous gases. The same idea can test drinking water, food, or medicine, because each material gives off its own colors when it is heated. Copper chloride is green. Strontium chloride is red. Sodium chloride is yellow. Those flame colors are a hint of the same idea: matter and light are paired.
