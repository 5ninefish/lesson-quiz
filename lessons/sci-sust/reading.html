About 15 minutes. Goal: say what a coral is, and why a reef matters.

Why this matters

Coral reefs feed people, shelter fish, and blunt waves before they reach shore. In Hawaiian tradition, coral is the first life in the Kumulipo, and the health of the reef mattered because people depended on reef fish. Ancient Hawaiians also understood coral as ancestral akua, deities that provide life.

Worked example

A coral polyp is an animal. It does not make its own food. Tiny algae called zooxanthellae live in its tissue. The algae make food by photosynthesis and share it. The coral gives them shelter and the wastes they use. That partnership is symbiosis. The polyp also has tentacles that capture food from the water. The algae do not turn the animal into a plant.

A reef is the accumulated skeleton of many coral animals, mostly calcium carbonate. Other species use the holes for shelter and the living tissue for food. The lesson’s sources put more than a quarter of marine life on reefs, and they warn that a large share of reefs are at risk.

About 18 minutes. Goal: tell common Hawaiian coral species apart by shape and place.

Why this matters

A reef is not one coral. Each species fills a different place. Some take waves. Some need calm water and strong sun. Knowing the species is how a map becomes useful.

Worked example

Lobe coral, Porites lobata, is the example, not one of the four matches below. It grows as a massive or encrusting head from the intertidal zone to deeper than 180 feet. The calices look like shallow snowflakes, flush with the surface. Living colonies are yellowish-green, brown, or sometimes blue. Long narrow cracks can be the work of a snapping shrimp. Hold that picture: a heavy head, not a bush of fingers.

The set on this reef

  • Rose coral, also called cauliflower, Pocillopora meandrina. The common Pocillopora in Hawaiʻi. It prefers wave-agitated water and is found to about 150 feet. Heads are about 10 to 20 inches, with heavy leaf-like branches and wart-like verrucae. Color runs from brown to pink.
  • Lace coral, Pocillopora damicornis. Delicate clumps to about 6 inches. Fine branches are slender in calm water. It likes protected reef flats, depends on sunlight, and is rarely found below about 30 feet.
  • Antler coral, Pocillopora eydouxi. Thick pipe-like branches, usually from about 35 to 150 feet, and usually a darker brown.
  • Finger coral, Porites compressa. Finger-like branches and snowflake calices. Most common in wave-protected bays or on deeper slopes, to about 150 feet.
  • Rice coral, Montipora capitata. Nipple-like papillae with no calices on the papillae themselves. The surface looks like rice and pepper. Usually brown, to about 150 feet. A plate can have white edges.
  • Mushroom coral, Fungia scutaria. One polyp, free-living, often on the reef flat between cracks. The disk is about 1.5 to 7 inches. Young ones grow on a stalk, then break free.
  • Cup coral, Tubastraea coccinea. Clumps about 2 to 4 inches, often bright orange, pink, or black. That color is not from zooxanthellae. It lives in shade: ledges, caves, and shady tidepools. It does not build the reef frame.

About 18 minutes. Goal: tell a chemical change from a soak in water, and name the pH scale.

Why this matters

What people do on land reaches the reef. The lesson names runoff from vehicles, gardens, and sewage, and hotter water. In Hawaiʻi, sewage is one of the biggest threats the lesson cites. You do not need to mix anything on this page.

Worked example

The classroom uses chalk as a stand-in because chalk is calcium carbonate, the same material as a coral skeleton. In plain water, the chalk still looks like chalk after a short soak. That is not a chemical change. An acid is different. Acidity is read on the pH scale: 7 is neutral, lower than 7 is an acid, higher than 7 is a base. When an acid reacts with calcium carbonate, the piece changes and you do not get the same piece back. A chemical change is usually hard to undo. Signs can include a new smell, bubbles, or heat. This page does not ask you to pour an acid, oil, soap, or weed killer.

No explainer should show a mixing recipe. The idea is the change, not the supplies.

About 20 minutes. Goal: write a research card and choose how to scan it.

Why this matters

Before the class builds a model, each group learns four things about one species: its role, who depends on it, the conditions it needs, and where it is prominent. Then they map the tabletop reef. A scan is a simpler cousin of the tools scientists use underwater.

Worked example

Take lobe coral as the card, since you already know it. Role: a massive head is a foundation other coral can grow on, and fish use the cracks. Who depends on it: reef fish and the shrimp in those cracks. Conditions: it handles shallow water and deep water, from the intertidal zone past 180 feet. Where: Hawaiian reefs, including places with strong light and places far down.

The June deck offers two scan modes. Mesh fits a model you want to move into another app, with no background and more precise measurements. Splat fits a photorealistic scene, background included, over a larger range. Either way, move slowly, cover the same spot more than once, and get above and beneath the object.

The lesson describes Scaniverse as a mix of LiDAR and photogrammetry. LiDAR sends light and times the bounce to get distance. Photogrammetry stacks a large number of photos into one shape. You do not need the app to learn the difference.

About 12 minutes. Goal: say what changes in the form, and what stays the species.

Why this matters

A dive note that says “only one species” can make a reef sound poor. A wider look can show that one species is growing in several forms, and those forms still make different homes.

Worked example

Porites lobata and Porites compressa are close relatives. Lobe coral is often massive. Finger coral branches, and it prefers wave-protected water. The June deck puts lobe forms on the top row and finger forms below, and the note says the morphology differs because the places differ. Wave, depth, sunlight, flow, and neighbors can change the shape. The species name stays.

Lace coral shows the same idea inside one species. Branches are slender in calm water and sturdier where waves hit. It needs sun and is rarely found below about 30 feet. Finger coral’s home is the protected bay or a deeper slope.

About 12 minutes. Goal: say why a map helps, and what a before-and-after picture can show.

Why this matters

Scientists map reefs so they can watch health, see how a place reacts to a real event such as runoff, and read which species are there. A scan will not replace a dive. It covers a weakness of one swim.

Worked example

On a dive, a person writes while swimming against a current and can study only a small patch at a time. A 3D model lets you look across a slope later, including cracks a photo flattens. The lesson also shows before-and-after pictures: look for the number of fish, the color, and whether living coral gave way to pale or dead patches. Causes the lesson names include storms, a changing climate, and pollution. A limit stays: a scan is one moment, and it misses fish that swim out of the frame. Maps and dives work together. Some restoration grows coral and outplants it. Printed structures are a different tool: the idea in this lesson is that marine life can live on them, not that they replace a living reef.
